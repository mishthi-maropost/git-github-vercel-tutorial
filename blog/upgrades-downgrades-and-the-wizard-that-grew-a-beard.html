Upgrades, Downgrades, and the Wizard That Grew a Beard

Some designers get to make things delightful. I get to make things survivable — specifically, the moment a customer tries to give us less money, and the moment a campaign builder tries to show them seventeen things at once. Here’s a tour through my last few months of billing chaos, multichannel mayhem, and a pricing model that made me learn HubSpot’s homework.

Mishthi

The Upgrade/Downgrade Saga, or: How to Break Up Gracefully

Nobody designs an upgrade flow and cries about it. Everybody designs a downgrade flow and questions their career choices. I’ve been leading the redesign of Maropost’s subscription lifecycle, which is a fancy way of saying I spent weeks thinking very hard about how to let someone leave without it feeling like a hostage negotiation.

We’re moving Marketing Cloud onto Active Profiles pricing, and rebuilding the whole Subscriptions section around a split I’m honestly proud of: Health, Growth, and Management — basically the Mind, Body, and Spirit of your subscription. Health tells you how your account’s doing. Growth tempts you to spend more, gently, like a menu with the profiteroles conveniently at eye level. Management is where you go to do the scary stuff.

And the scary stuff needed guardrails. We made downgrades Sales-only, because letting someone self-serve their way down a pricing tier at 11pm on a Tuesday felt like letting someone text their ex after two drinks — technically possible, rarely wise. Cancellation lives in what we now call the Danger Zone, which is exactly as ominous as it sounds, and also exactly the emotional register that button deserves. It took ten prototypes to get there. Ten. I’ve had shorter relationships.

Building a Wizard, Taming a Report

Then there’s the Multichannel Campaign experience — half report, half campaign-building wizard, fully a lesson in humility.

The report side is the easy part to explain: five tabs — Overview, Email, SMS, Overlay, Details — walking a marketer from “how did my campaign do” to “how did it really do” once you account for people who got hit by both email and SMS. We kept the whole thing in a strict monochrome palette with Inter as the only font, because when you’re showing someone data about why their SMS numbers were softer than expected, this is not the moment for a confetti color scheme.

The wizard side is where things got feral. Picture the audience-selection step of the campaign builder trying to display seven different chip groups at once — Segments, Tables, Lists, plus Suppress Lists, Suppress Journeys, Suppress Segments, and Suppress Secure Lists — like a group project where everyone insists on presenting their slide. The old solution was a “+10 more / −10 less” paginator, which is the UX equivalent of reading a phone book ten digits at a time. I proposed collapsing it down to “+X more ▼” showing the first three items, because marketers building a campaign at 4:45pm on a Friday do not need to audit their entire suppression architecture chip by chip. They need to hit send and go home.

Marketing Contacts: Teaching an Old Paywall New Tricks

And finally, Marketing Contacts — our answer to “wait, how many people are we actually paying to email?” We’re introducing a Monthly Active Marketing Contacts model, which if you squint is basically doing what HubSpot did, except we get to have opinions about it now too.

The tricky part wasn’t the pricing logic, it was slotting a genuinely new concept into an existing paywall that already had a whole personality — plan cards, feature lists, a well-worn path to Chargebee for payment. It’s less “build a new room” and more “convince the load-bearing wall it wants a new window.” So far the wall is cooperating. Mostly.

The Actual Moral

If there’s a theme across billing, wizards, and contact math, it’s that the least visible parts of a product are the ones people feel the most — a downgrade button, a chip that won’t stop multiplying, a number that suddenly determines your bill. I don’t get to make any of it fun, exactly. But I can make it make sense, and honestly, at this point, I’ll take “made sense” as a personal win.

Core takeaway

The least visible parts of a product are the ones people feel the most.
